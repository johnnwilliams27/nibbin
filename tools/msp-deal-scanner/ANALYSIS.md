_Companion memo to the scanner. The tables in `report.md` are generated; this is the judgment
behind the ranking. Every financial cited is an **unverified** first-pass capture from public
marketplace summaries — treat it as a lead to confirm, not a fact. Not investment advice._

The scanner ranks by **fit to your Deal-1 buy box**, not by cheapness. A deal is only good if it
is the *right* business bought the *right* way: a $1–1.5M EBITDA, onshore, 90%+-recurring,
GM-run, professional-services-leaning MSP at ≤4.5×, with a retiring owner carrying paper — one
you can re-rate from ~3× to 8–12× by bolting on tuck-ins and taking L1 out with AI. Read against
that, most of what's publicly listed in Texas is noise, a handful are worth a CIM, and the single
best *business* on the board is probably the wrong *Deal 1*.

## 1. What actually creates value in an MSP roll-up (the macro lens)

A good market pays for **durable, transferable, expandable cash flow** — and punishes everything
that looks like the owner's personal book of hours. In rough order of multiple impact:

1. **Contractual MRR mix.** The percentage of revenue under recurring per-seat managed contracts
   vs. project / time-and-materials / hardware resale. 90%+ MRR is the gold standard and the
   single biggest multiple driver. Break/fix and resale are low-multiple because they don't
   recur and don't compound.
2. **Retention & concentration.** 85%+ gross retention, ideally >100% net (seat expansion), no
   client over ~15%, 20+ logos. Diversified, sticky revenue is what a consolidator underwrites.
3. **Management depth / owner-independence.** A GM or service manager who *owns the client
   relationships* is the difference between a financeable, re-ratable platform and a job you
   bought. This is why it's the plan's one non-negotiable. Owner-as-everything = tuck-in at best.
4. **The AI margin lever — and where it lives.** Industry net margins run 10–15%. The re-rate to
   ~35% comes from agentic ticket triage, auto-documentation, and L1 auto-resolution. Crucially,
   **that uplift is largest in an onshore shop still paying full-cost humans to do L1** — those
   are the salaries AI removes. A shop that already cut L1 cost some other way has less headroom
   left for you to capture.
5. **Vertical concentration on a generalist chassis.** A defensible niche (legal, professional
   services) with reference logos and domain tooling compounds via referrals and commands a
   premium — but you keep a generalist chassis so you're not a single-vertical bet. Chassis
   quality beats vertical fit for Deal 1.
6. **Stack hygiene.** A standardized RMM/PSA/documentation stack (ConnectWise/Datto/NinjaOne +
   IT Glue) makes tuck-ins integratable; snowflake stacks carry real integration cost and
   dilute synergy.
7. **Security & compliance capability.** Real security services (not AV resale) are the
   fastest-growing, highest-margin, stickiest MSP line — worth a premium and a natural sell into
   a legal/professional-services base.
8. **Density & the synergy itself.** Roll-up value is manufactured in the back office: one NOC,
   one helpdesk, one toolset, one finance function spread across tuck-ins bought at 2.5–3.5×.
   Geographic density supports the relationship + dispatch model. The flywheel = multiple
   arbitrage on entry × EBITDA growth from tuck-ins × margin expansion from AI + shared services,
   realized at an 8–12× exit.

**The 2026 bullseye:** ~$1–1.5M EBITDA, DFW-metro, 90%+ MRR, <15% concentration, GM-run,
standardized stack, professional-services tilt, priced ≤4.5× with a retiring owner carrying
20–40%. Nothing currently *confirmed* on the public Texas board is all of that — which is itself
the finding (see §5).

## 2. The onshore vs. offshore question, at macro scale

You asked me to actually reason this out rather than reflex-penalize it. Both sides are real.

**The case *for* offshore delivery (generic MSP economics).** Labor is 50–60% of an MSP's cost
base. An offshore NOC/helpdesk can lift gross margin 15–30 points, fund 24/7 follow-the-sun
coverage cheaply, and scale L1/L2 without US wage exposure. Plenty of profitable MSPs run this
way, and on a pure earnings-multiple screen an offshore shop often looks *cheaper* because the
margin is already fat. If your strategy were "cost leader, price-competitive, non-sensitive SMB
verticals," offshore could be a genuine edge.

**Why it's the wrong buy for *this* plan specifically — four structural reasons:**

1. **It fights the vertical.** Legal and professional-services clients are confidentiality- and
   compliance-sensitive. Law firms carry privilege, client-confidentiality, and increasingly
   data-residency obligations; "your matter data is supported offshore" is a trust liability and
   sometimes a hard client constraint — the opposite of a selling point in the exact vertical the
   plan is built to win.
2. **It cannibalizes your own AI thesis.** Offshore is the *old* way to take cost out of L1. AI
   is the *new* way, and it's the plan's differentiator. Buy an offshore cost base and (a) that
   margin arbitrage is already priced into the earnings you're paying ~4× for, and (b) you can't
   stack AI savings on labor you're already paying pennies for. You *want* the onshore shop with
   expensive, automatable L1 — that's where your value-creation lever has the most room.
3. **Transferability & fragility.** Offshore teams often sit in a contractor entity, a foreign
   subsidiary, or the seller's personal relationships abroad. Change-of-control retention is
   riskier; add FX, rising offshore wage inflation, time-zone management overhead, and
   geopolitical/visa exposure. Diligence and post-close integration are materially harder.
4. **It compresses the exit multiple.** The whole plan is multiple arbitrage — buy ~3×, sell
   8–12×. Strategic and PE consolidators pay the top of that range for *clean, onshore,
   contracted, low-concentration, GM-run US MSPs* and discount offshore-heavy delivery for
   integration and client-consent risk. Anything that shaves the exit multiple is strategically
   expensive even when entry looks cheap.

**Verdict:** for the EMPIRE thesis, a cheap offshore multiple is not a bargain — it's fair-to-rich
once you discount for lower earnings quality and the exit-multiple haircut. That is exactly why
the scanner puts the **Plano "Off-Shore IT Services / Managed Services"** deal in **Caution**,
even though its numbers are identical to a Platform-tier listing. Which brings us to the tell:
the offshore listing and the statewide **"IT - Mass Service Provider"** share the *exact* same
$4.25M price and $1,039,880 cash flow — almost certainly the same business, one framed onshore
and one offshore. If it's genuinely offshore-delivered, the onshore framing is marketing; if it's
onshore, it's a real Deal-1 candidate. **Resolving that one question is the highest-value
diligence item on the board.**

## 2b. The capital stack, and the two levers you asked about

**Structure 1 — the financeable envelope.** $5M SBA 7(a) (Prime + 2.5–3%, 10-yr am) + a $0.7–1M
seller note (10–15%, on 2-yr full standby) + $0.5–0.7M of your cash ≈ a **~$6.7M business price**
(the ~$6.5–7M in your note). At 4.75–5× that's the **$1.4–1.5M-EBITDA business with real
management** you're aiming at, with debt service ~$800–850K/yr against $1.4–1.5M EBITDA — leaving
$550–650K of cash flow to cover your $250K draw plus $300–400K of annual debt sweep. The scanner
now scores every candidate against this $6.7M envelope, not a flat multiple.

**Lever 1 — seller financing (use it; it does double duty).** Because half your required 10%
equity injection can be seller *standby* paper, a seller note both **stretches your cash** (you're
not writing the whole equity check) and **signals conviction** — a seller carrying 15% on standby
for two years is betting on the business surviving their exit, which is information. So a deal with
seller financing available can support a *higher* price at the same cash-in than one without.
Treat "will the seller carry paper, and how much?" as a top-3 question on every candidate; where
it's unconfirmed (all of the current board), it's an open variable that changes what you can
afford. It is a reason to *lean in* on an otherwise-great business, not a nicety.

**Lever 2 — real estate (only if it makes a great business pencil).** If an owned building comes
attached, finance it on a **separate 504 / conventional CRE loan (~85% LTV) outside the 7(a)** —
it lifts total deal capacity *without* eating the $6.7M business envelope, and you hold a hard,
separately-collateralized asset. But you're right to be wary: MSPs are asset-light and you don't
want to become a landlord for its own sake. Take the real estate only when it's the thing that
makes an otherwise-great, otherwise-too-big deal financeable, or when owning beats leasing on the
math. **None of the current Texas board includes real estate** — every listing is service/remote —
so this lever is dormant today. The scanner already models it (`realEstate` field → separate
loan), so the moment an RE-inclusive MSP appears, it prices the blended structure automatically.

**So does anything match the $1.4–1.5M "real-management" profile?** Closest is **Brazos** (a bit
*above* it at $1.7M, financeable only at a disciplined price — see §3). **Houston-contracts** and
the **cybersecurity** shop are the unknowns that *could* land in the window — get their numbers.
Everything else confirmed is smaller (financeable, but under-using your capacity) or off-thesis.
The honest read: the exact $1.4–1.5M-EBITDA, GM-run, professional-services MSP at ≤5× is a deal
you'll more likely *originate off-market* than find on a marketplace, because on-market ones get
bid to 5–8×.

## 4. What this board *doesn't* have (the real conclusion)

Run the buy box honestly and the punchline is that **the public Texas marketplaces are not where
your Deal 1 lives.** The three Platform-tier hits are a possibly-duplicated statewide/offshore
listing, a Round-Rock shop whose price you can't trust, and Brazos — the best business, now
financeable under Structure 1 but only if the seller carries paper and prices below what a
consolidator would pay. Everything else is a tuck-in or a pass. That's not a failure of the
search — it's the structural reality that the clean, GM-run, $1.4–1.5M-EBITDA, professional-services
MSP with a retiring owner willing to sell at ≤5× rarely hits BizBuySell. Those transact through specialist advisors
(Cogent, Evergreen, CT Acquisitions) and, more often, through **direct owner outreach** — which is
exactly why the plan has you building broker lists and sending owner letters from 2028. The
marketplace scan is for calibration and the occasional gem; the proprietary pipeline is where
Deal 1 actually comes from.
